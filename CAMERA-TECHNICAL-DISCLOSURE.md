## Claim I. Embodied Interaction System from a Single RGB Camera

A unified real-time system in which GPU-accelerated (for example via WebGPU compute shaders) machine learning (ML) inference extracts one or more of head position, hand landmarks, or other body-relative spatial signals from a single consumer camera feed, including but not limited to a built-in webcam, and uses these signals to drive one or more interactive modalities including head-coupled visual parallax, bilateral hand tracking with derived depth, or other vision-derived interaction, all running concurrently in an unmodified browser tab or comparable client-side runtime on commodity hardware. Conventional six-degrees-of-freedom (6DOF) VR requires either a headset with onboard tracking cameras, an external depth sensor, or a multi-camera rig. This system synthesizes equivalent interaction from signals already present in a single RGB stream, including but not limited to a consumer webcam. No prior system is known to combine these elements in this configuration as of the date of this disclosure.

In one embodiment, the head-coupled parallax pipeline provides three translational and three rotational degrees of freedom from face landmarks or other facial feature detection methods. Adding depth-estimated hand position may extend this into full-body interactive space. A user can, for example, lean left to peer around a virtual object, reach toward the screen and feel their hand move into the scene, and pull back to bring it forward again. The physical metaphor is reaching through a window, not wearing a headset. This interaction model is fully implemented and demonstrated in a related private repository as of the date of this disclosure.

GPU-accelerated inference at interactive frame rates (for example 120fps or higher) driving one or more of head-coupled parallax, hand tracking, depth estimation, or other vision-derived interaction modalities, running concurrently in a browser tab or comparable client-side runtime, may produce a first-person interactive 3D experience requiring no hardware beyond the camera built into any modern laptop or similar consumer device.

### I.A. Head-Coupled Visual Parallax with Concurrent Hand Gesture Input

A single RGB camera feed (for example via `getUserMedia` or another video capture API) is shared between two or more concurrent ML inference pipelines (where "inference" refers to the process of running a trained neural network on camera frames to produce spatial predictions such as landmark coordinates) running in separate execution contexts, such as Web Workers, threads, or processes. Face detection extracts head position from facial keypoints, including but not limited to eye landmarks (for example, midpoint for x/y, inter-eye distance for z depth proxy). Hand landmark detection extracts 3D landmarks (for example 21 per hand per frame) for gesture recognition. Inference contexts may receive video frames via zero-copy transfer mechanisms such as `ImageBitmap` via `postMessage`, shared memory, or other efficient frame-sharing approaches. ML inference may run partially or entirely off the main rendering thread, preserving smooth interactive rendering at rates including 120fps or higher.

The 3D scene (rendered using any suitable 3D engine, such as Three.js, WebGL, WebGPU, Unity, or Unreal Engine) applies head position as camera translation, creating the illusion of looking through a window into 3D space. Forward and backward head movement, derived from facial feature geometry such as inter-eye distance, maps to camera Z translation in the 3D scene. The viewer physically leans in to move deeper into the space, and leans back to retreat. At sufficiently high frame rates and sufficiently low tracking latency (see Claim II), this produces a direct perceptual experience of scene traversal requiring no controller input. Hand gestures may simultaneously trigger interactive events, deepening the experience of embodied spatial presence. The same head-coupled parallax and gesture input techniques may be applied to 2D rendering contexts, for example driving differential layer scrolling in a 2D scene or controlling a sprite's position, as well as to 3D scenes.

The specific combination of one or more vision-derived interactive modalities, including but not limited to head-coupled parallax rendering and hand gesture input, driven by concurrent off-thread ML inference from a single consumer RGB camera, running entirely in a client-side runtime such as a browser without plugins or depth sensors, is a novel system architecture as of the date of this disclosure.

### I.B. Head Position as 3D Gameplay Input

The same head position signal that drives parallax camera translation may simultaneously serve as the player's spatial position within the 3D game world. The player's tracked head position may map to a player hitbox, avatar position, or other spatial representation in the scene, such that physical head movement produces corresponding movement in game space. For example, physically leaning left may move the player's position out of the path of an incoming projectile, leaning back may increase distance from an approaching threat, and leaning forward may close distance for a melee interaction. The head-driven game position and the head-driven parallax view may update from the same tracking signal, producing a unified experience in which the player sees the scene shift and simultaneously occupies a different position within it. This enables physical dodging, weaving, and spatial positioning as gameplay mechanics, driven entirely by natural body movement with no controller input. The player may be anchored to a fixed viewpoint and interact by leaning within the parallax window, or the player may move through the 3D space via any navigation mechanism, including but not limited to gesture-driven locomotion, gaze-directed movement, head-lean-to-walk, or other input mappings. The system supports both stationary and traversal interaction models.

### I.C. Parallax-Coupled Hand Gesture Coordinate System

Hand landmarks may be mapped to 3D scene space relative to the current parallax camera position. In this embodiment, the hand may ride with the head-coupled perspective shift. Most systems treat hand position and head tracking as independent coordinate systems. Coupling them makes the hand feel like it exists inside the parallax window rather than floating in front of it. This effect is central to producing coherent first-person interaction in a head-coupled parallax scene. Applying the same smoothing filter (for example a One Euro filter with matched parameters) to both the hand tracking and head tracking signals may preserve temporal coherence between the two coordinate systems, ensuring that smoothing-induced latency affects both signals equally and the coupled mapping remains aligned.

### I.D. Depth-Corrected Hand Projection for Desktop VR Interactions

Within the coupled coordinate system of I.C, hand landmark models, including but not limited to MediaPipe, may produce per-landmark z-values inferred from hand scale and proportions that describe depth within a scene. By mapping one or more depth values into the head-coupled parallax scene with an inverse projection correction, a hand physically moving toward the camera may be rendered as moving into the 3D scene rather than growing larger on a flat screen. Depth values may optionally be smoothed prior to mapping (for example via a One Euro filter or other adaptive low-pass filter) to reduce noise, but the core technique is the inverse projection mapping itself. This counteracts the natural 2D projection (where closer objects appear larger) and produces spatially coherent hand movement within the parallax space. For example, a punch toward the webcam reads as a punch into the screen.

The depth mapping may use smoothed absolute z-position, relative z-velocity (z-delta), or a combination of both, depending on the interaction type. For example, smoothed position may provide stable continuous placement for aiming and spatial interaction, while z-velocity derivatives may be used for momentum-dependent interactions such as throwing or striking. This enables desktop VR style interactions, including for example boxing, tennis, or other gesture-driven spatial interactions, from a single consumer webcam, without a depth sensor or headset.

### I.E. Stability-Aware Landmark Estimation

The landmark depth signal may degrade when landmarks are occluded or the hand partially exits the frame. A stability-aware selection mechanism, such as a visibility-weighted cascade, may select one or more anchor points based on stability criteria at each frame. On anchor transitions, the system may apply computed offsets or other continuity-preserving corrections to maintain positional continuity across any axis, including but not limited to depth (Z), lateral position (X, Y), or combinations thereof. More generally, the position of less stable landmarks may be estimated or constrained based on the positions of more stable landmarks, enabling the system to infer the likely location of degraded points from reliable ones. Together, these mechanisms may maintain continuous tracking even as the hand moves toward the edges of the camera's field of view. In aiming applications, for example, a finger pointed toward the camera foreshortens in the image, collapsing distal landmarks into a small cluster and degrading positional estimation precisely when accurate aim tracking is most needed. When such distal landmarks drop below a confidence threshold, the system may fall back to more proximal landmarks such as the wrist or palm base while preserving the last known positional estimates, maintaining continuous aim ray stability through high-uncertainty poses.

The estimation of degraded landmark positions may additionally incorporate temporal progression. The system may maintain a velocity history for each tracked landmark, and when a landmark's stability degrades, its predicted position may be derived from its own prior velocity, from the current velocity of one or more neighboring landmarks that remain stable, or from a combination of both. The predicted position may be computed using mathematical methods including but not limited to linear extrapolation, weighted averaging, linear regression, or other algebraic expressions that combine point stability metrics with previous known states. More generally, any technique that uses the temporal trajectory and spatial relationships of stable landmarks to predict the likely position of unstable landmarks falls within this approach.

This prediction and correction process may alternatively or additionally be provided as input to a secondary machine learning model trained to estimate corrected landmark positions from noisy or degraded inputs, using stable landmark positions, velocity histories, and confidence scores as features. Such a model may be trained on-device or pre-trained, and may operate as a post-processing stage within the tracking pipeline, providing learned landmark stabilization that adapts to patterns of degradation specific to the interaction context.

### I.F. Head-Coupled Aim via Multi-Point Spatial Mapping

In a head-coupled parallax scene, the apparent aim vector of a pointed finger changes as the viewer's head moves, because the ray from the viewer's eye through the fingertip originates from a different viewpoint. Leaning right shifts both the viewing origin and the viewing angle, and a finger pointed at a fixed screen location now traces a ray to a different point in 3D scene space. This system may derive a screen-space aim point, and optionally a continuous world-space aim vector, from the geometric relationship between tracked head position and tracked fingertip position, such that the aim output may update in real time as the head and/or hand move. The effect is analogous to physically looking through an aim scope: the viewer leans to align their perspective, and the system tracks where their finger points on the flat screen and may derive from this where they are actually aiming in the 3D scene. The resulting aim output may drive downstream interactions including but not limited to cursor movement, target selection, the rendering of a visible aim indicator such as a crosshair or projected ray, or any other interaction driven by a spatially derived vector.

Calibration may be performed by presenting one or more target indicators at known screen positions, using any spatial arrangement including but not limited to a static grid (for example a 5x4 grid spanning 5% to 95% of both axes), sequential points, scattered positions, or a moving target that traverses the screen. The user may point at each target and perform a capture action such as a pinch, dwell, or other gesture to record a sample. Each sample records the known screen coordinate as ground truth alongside the tracked hand reference point (including but not limited to a fingertip, palm center, palm normal vector, or other hand-derived spatial indicator) and head position from the tracking pipeline. The calibration sequence may cycle or repeat, allowing additional passes at different head positions to improve robustness across viewing angles. Samples indicating lost or degraded tracking may be discarded before fitting.

From the collected samples, one or more regression models or other learned mappings from pointer and head position to screen coordinates are fit in-browser (for example, two independent regressions, one for each screen axis), taking effect immediately with no page reload. Multiple model variants may be supported, from full models incorporating depth terms to simplified models using only 2D pointer and head position.

The regression output passes through a cascade of smoothing filters with tunable parameters, suppressing jitter on slow movements while preserving fast intentional aim changes.

The calibration mechanic uses the same gesture and tracking pipeline that drives all other system interactions. No additional hardware, no separate calibration device, no page reload. The system calibrates itself using its own input modality, and the resulting aim ray is continuously updated from both head and hand position, producing a world-space interaction ray that responds to physical viewing angle the way a real aimed instrument would.

### I.G. Eye-Gaze Tracking and Gaze-Driven Interaction from Facial Landmarks

The face landmark model used for head-coupled parallax may additionally produce eye landmark positions from which gaze direction can be derived, including but not limited to iris center position, eye corner positions, or other ocular landmarks. By tracking the position and orientation of the eyes relative to the head, the system may estimate where on the screen or within the 3D scene the user is looking, providing a gaze signal at no additional sensing cost beyond the existing face tracking pipeline.

Gaze-driven interactions may operate without calibration, using raw relative gaze direction (for example iris position relative to eye corners) for region-level detection such as determining whether the user is looking at the top, center, or bottom of the screen. For applications requiring higher precision, the calibration exercise described in I.F may simultaneously capture gaze calibration data: because the user's eyes naturally fixate on the tracking target, each calibration sample may additionally record the estimated gaze direction alongside the hand reference point and head position, producing a gaze-to-screen mapping as a byproduct of the hand-aiming calibration. The user need not be aware that gaze calibration is occurring.

The resulting gaze signal may drive downstream interactions including but not limited to: foveated rendering, in which visual detail or computational resources are concentrated in the region where the user is looking and reduced in the periphery; gaze-aware UI elements that respond to being looked at, for example highlighting, expanding, or activating when the user's gaze dwells on them, such as a contextual menu or toolbar that appears when the user's gaze enters or dwells upon a screen region and recedes when gaze moves away; attention-driven game mechanics in which looking at an object triggers an in-game effect; or any other interaction driven by estimated gaze direction. Foveated rendering in particular has historically required dedicated eye-tracking hardware such as that found in VR headsets. Deriving a comparable gaze signal from the same webcam face landmarks already used for head-coupled parallax, at no additional hardware or pipeline cost, extends this capability to commodity devices.

The gaze signal may be used independently, in combination with the hand-derived aim signal of I.F, or as a fallback when hand tracking is unavailable or degraded. For example, the system may use gaze for coarse target acquisition and hand pointing for fine aim refinement, or may switch between modalities based on tracking confidence.

The estimated gaze direction may additionally be fed back into the head-coupled parallax camera system. Where head position drives camera translation (Claim I), gaze direction may drive a complementary camera adjustment, including but not limited to camera rotation, camera translation offset, or a combination thereof. In one embodiment, a subtle inverse camera movement based on gaze direction may cause the point the user is looking at to drift slightly toward the center of the screen, producing an attentional gravity effect in which the scene continuously recenters around the user's focus point. This recentering may be imperceptible to the user while producing measurable enhancement of perceived depth: the gaze-driven camera offset creates differential motion between foreground and background objects near the gaze point, synthesizing motion parallax cues that reinforce depth perception. Because the camera movement tracks the user's attention, it moves with rather than against their perceptual expectations, reducing or eliminating the motion discomfort that arbitrary camera rotation might produce.

The combination of head-driven camera translation and gaze-driven camera adjustment may produce up to six degrees of freedom from a single webcam: three translational axes from head position and up to three rotational or translational axes from gaze direction, without a headset or additional sensors. The intensity of gaze-coupled camera adjustment may be user-tunable or adaptive, ranging from imperceptible depth enhancement to full gaze-directed look control. Smoothing of the transformations applied via these signals may or may not be applied, depending on the desired responsiveness and perceptual effect.

### I.H. Physiological Viewport Modulation from Facial State

The face landmark pipeline described in this disclosure produces blendshape coefficients that encode the state of the user's eyelids, brow, and other facial features in real time. These signals, including but not limited to eyelid closure (for example eyeBlinkLeft, eyeBlinkRight), squint intensity (for example eyeSquintLeft, eyeSquintRight), and brow position, may be used to modulate the player's own viewport rather than, or in addition to, driving avatar animation.

For example, when the system detects the user squinting, it may apply graduated darkening or vignette effects at the top and bottom of the screen, emulating the visual narrowing produced by partially closed eyelids. When the user's eyes widen, the vignette may recede, which may produce a sensation of visual opening. The screen may respond to the user's own facial state, reinforcing the perceptual experience of embodiment within the rendered space.

Detected facial states may additionally drive gameplay mechanics. For example, a sustained squint detected via eyelid blendshape coefficients may trigger a camera zoom or field-of-view narrowing, functioning as a physical aim-down-sights mechanic: the player physically squints to focus, and the viewport responds by zooming in on the aim point derived from I.F or I.G. This maps a natural physical reflex (squinting to focus on a distant target) to a gameplay action (precision aiming), creating an interaction that may feel instinctive rather than learned. More generally, any sustained or transient facial expression may be mapped to a discrete or continuous gameplay trigger, including but not limited to zoom, selection via blink, mode switching, ability activation, or environmental interaction.

More generally, any detected facial state may be mapped to a corresponding viewport modification, including but not limited to: vignette intensity modulated by eyelid closure, color temperature or saturation shifts driven by brow tension or other expressions of affect, depth-of-field adjustments driven by squint or focus indicators, or any other visual post-processing effect driven by real-time facial state. The facial signals are already present in the tracking pipeline at no additional sensing cost. This approach extends the embodiment chain established by head-coupled parallax (Claim I), gaze tracking (I.G), and spatial audio (I.M) into a further modality: the player's own face modulates their visual experience of the scene.

### I.I. Fused Monocular Depth from Orthogonal Cues

In a system already tracking both head position and hand position from a single camera for parallax and gesture input, the geometric relationship between these two tracked points provides a second depth cue at zero additional sensing cost. As the user's head shifts, the angular relationship between head and hand in camera space changes as a function of true 3D distance, encoding geometric depth independent of the landmark depth signal. Fusing both signals, for example using confidence weighting, may produce depth estimates that are robust when either signal is noisy or degraded. The system's existing dual-tracking architecture produces orthogonal depth cues as a byproduct of its primary functions, requiring no additional sensor, model, or pipeline stage. More generally, this approach encompasses any technique that fuses two or more monocular depth cues already present in the system to produce a combined depth estimate. Such cues may include but are not limited to landmark spread, face-relative angular geometry, apparent size, or other camera-derived signals.

### I.J. Real-Time Facial Animation with Geometry-Driven Lip Synchronization

A dedicated blendshape inference model running on a selected subset of face landmarks (for example 146 landmarks) produces a set of blendshape coefficients compatible with a standard blendshape convention (for example 52 ARKit-compatible coefficients) at the tracking frame rate. These coefficients drive morph target deformation on any 3D character mesh rigged with the corresponding blendshape targets, enabling real-time facial puppeting of stylized avatars from webcam input alone. The blendshape inference worker runs fire-and-forget with one frame of latency, adding no fps overhead to the tracking pipeline.

Two approaches to lip synchronization are supported. In geometry-driven lip sync, mouth blendshape coefficients including jawOpen, mouthClose, mouthFunnel, mouthPucker, mouthSmileLeft/Right, and related coefficients are derived from the physical positions of lip and jaw landmarks as observed by the camera. Mouth animation reflects actual jaw movement rather than audio amplitude. A person mouthing words silently produces accurate lip animation. This approach may produce geometrically faithful mouth animation that audio-amplitude-driven lip sync cannot achieve.

In audio-corroborated lip sync, geometry-driven mouth coefficients are cross-referenced with real-time audio amplitude from the microphone, obtained for example via getUserMedia audio track processed through an AnalyserNode or any equivalent audio analysis mechanism. When speech is detected but mouth blendshapes show minimal movement the system boosts mouth animation. When mouth movement is detected without audio the system identifies silent gesturing. This dual-signal approach disambiguates speaking from non-speaking mouth movement in ways neither signal alone can achieve.

The geometry-driven approach is a key differentiator from existing VTubing and avatar animation software, which typically drives jawOpen or equivalent mouth parameters from audio amplitude alone. This system derives multiple distinct mouth blendshape coefficients (for example 12 or more) from real facial geometry, capturing lip shape, jaw position, and mouth configuration independently of whether the user is speaking.

### I.K. Compact Networked Player State for Browser-Based Facial Presence

Browser-based multiplayer games typically lack facial presence: the real-time representation of a player's facial expressions, head movement, and lip articulation on a remote player's screen. Players are instead represented by usernames, static avatars, or at most voice chat. Real-time facial animation has historically required dedicated hardware such as VR headsets with face tracking sensors, professional motion capture rigs, or bandwidth-intensive video streams, and is therefore rarely available in browser-based interactive contexts.

In the disclosed system, the face landmark pipeline already produces blendshape coefficients as part of its tracking architecture for head-coupled parallax and geometry-driven lip sync (I.J). Transmitting these coefficients to a remote peer is a zero-cost addition: the data already exists, and the complete player state including blendshapes, head pose, and hand landmarks can be as compact as for example under 1KB per frame. At 30fps this is approximately 22KB/sec over a real-time transport channel such as a WebRTC data channel, WebSocket, or other low-latency protocol, using less bandwidth than a standard voice call.

Geometry-driven lip synchronization (I.J) requires that blendshape coefficients reflecting physical jaw and lip movement arrive within the perceptual threshold for audio-visual synchronization. Research has established that lip sync delays beyond approximately 45ms are detectable, delays beyond 80ms are noticeable to most viewers, and film-quality synchronization requires alignment within approximately 22ms (ITU-R BT.1359-1; ATSC Implementation Subcommittee, 2003).

Benchmarked on identical hardware (see Claim II.A), the leading browser-based framework produces face landmarks and blendshape coefficients at 17.7ms latency for a single face, which meets the 22ms film-quality lip sync threshold in isolation, but simultaneously dropping rendering framerate to 56fps, below the 60fps minimum floor established for interactive applications where input-to-display responsiveness is perceptually critical (Claypool et al., 2023, ACM CHI; see also Claim II). The prior framework cannot achieve acceptable lip sync latency and acceptable rendering framerate simultaneously, even for a single face. When concurrent tasks are added, such as a second face or hand tracking, the prior framework's non-reentrant architecture serializes all tasks through a single WebGL context, increasing latency to 32.7ms and 37.8ms respectively, well past the detection threshold, while framerate drops further to 31fps and 27fps. The GPU-accelerated inference pipeline described in Claim II produces the same blendshape output at 11.1ms for a single face and approximately 12.8ms even under full concurrent workload, at 80fps and 71fps respectively, remaining within both film-quality synchronization thresholds and perceptual comfort framerate thresholds regardless of task count. This enables mouth animation that is tightly coupled to physical jaw movement rather than derived from audio amplitude.

As of the date of this disclosure, no known browser-based system uses WebGPU-accelerated inference to produce real-time blendshape coefficients for networked facial presence. The result is that full avatar facial presence, traditionally reserved for VR or studio environments, becomes a byproduct of the existing tracking pipeline rather than an additional system.

The GPU-accelerated inference pipeline, gesture classification system, and deterministic simulation architecture described in this document are not limited to desktop or laptop environments. The device screen serves as the parallax window, with head-coupled perspective shift driven by the front-facing camera's view of the user's face, and the free hand interacting in the 3D scene via the same landmark detection and feature classification pipeline.

The transition between one-hand and two-hand interaction modes may be automatic, driven by the hand detection pipeline's count of visible hands.

Multiple devices operating in a shared physical space may enable co-located multiplayer configurations. The rear-facing camera of a mobile device may serve as an AR viewport: the camera feed captures the physical environment while the system composites game content, such as spell effects, particle systems, or other interactive elements, over the real-world view. Combined with head-coupled parallax from the front-facing camera (or device orientation sensors), the rendered content may be spatially registered to the physical space, producing an augmented reality experience from a standard mobile device without requiring dedicated AR hardware, AR frameworks, or platform-specific AR APIs. The same GPU compute pipeline that drives gesture recognition and deterministic simulation renders the composited AR output.

The result is that the complete system, including head tracking, hand gesture classification, on-device ML training, deterministic multiplayer simulation, and AR compositing, may run on a mobile device in any client-side runtime, extending the architecture to mobile with no fundamental change to the pipeline and no native app installation required.

### I.M. Translational 6DOF Spatial Audio from Monocular Head Tracking

The same face-tracking data that drives head-coupled visual parallax may simultaneously drive a spatial audio listener position, for example via the Web Audio API AudioListener or any equivalent spatial audio system. Each frame, the listener coordinates may be set from the parallax camera position, which is itself derived from webcam-tracked facial features such as eye midpoint and inter-eye distance. When the user physically leans left, both the visual perspective and the audio perspective may shift together: audio sources pan compensatorily rightward, remaining world-locked in the 3D scene rather than fixed to the listener's head. This approach couples two normally independent output modalities, visual parallax and spatial audio, from a single face-tracking pipeline in real time.

Because the listener position updates with physical head movement, additional per-source audio effects that depend on source-to-listener distance and velocity may produce correlated perceptual changes that reinforce the spatial illusion. For example, per-source Doppler shift may be computed each frame by projecting the source velocity vector onto the source-to-listener unit vector to obtain radial velocity and applying a frequency scaling formula to the source signal. The Doppler ratio may be applied independently to two or more parallel signal paths (for example pitch and timbre separately), and the effective speed of sound may be user-tunable. Per-source spectral unmasking may progressively open a frequency-dependent filter as a source approaches the listener, for example decreasing the cutoff frequency of a high-pass filter inversely with distance, which may produce the sensation that an approaching object gains physical mass rather than merely gaining volume.

The combination of head-tracked panning, Doppler shift, and spectral unmasking, all driven by the same head-tracked listener position, may produce correlated changes across four or more perceptual dimensions simultaneously, including visual position, binaural pan, pitch, timbre, and spectral mass, from a single face-tracking pipeline.

### I.N. Summary-Driven Spatial Audio from Simulation State

In scenes containing dozens, hundreds, or thousands of localized sound-producing events (for example elemental interactions across a voxel simulation grid), instantiating a discrete audio source per event may exceed the practical limits of the audio processing system. This disclosure describes an alternative approach in which spatial audio may be generated from summary statistics of the simulation state rather than from individual point sources.

The simulation grid or scene state may be spatially subdivided at one or more levels of granularity, and summary information may be computed for each region, including but not limited to the dominant element types, aggregate intensity, average direction of motion, distance from the listener, and overall activity level. This summary information, which may correspond to the meta grid hierarchy described in Claim VII or any equivalent spatial summary, may then drive audio synthesis or sample triggering to produce a spatial sound field representative of the activity in each region. The audio generation may use any combination of techniques including but not limited to: triggering of audio samples at varying playback speeds, synthesis of filtered noise, granular synthesis, or other generative audio methods.

The summary-driven approach is not limited to the user's visual field. Activity occurring outside the current view but within audible range may contribute to the spatial audio output, providing the listener with awareness of off-screen events through sound.

Doppler effects may be computed from the primary motion vector of sound-generating elements within each summarized region, using the same velocity projection technique described in I.M, applied to the aggregate motion rather than to individual sources. Distance-dependent spectral filtering may be implemented by pre-assigning a set of filters to varying depth ranges in the scene, such that elements or content farther from the listener may be rendered with reduced high-frequency content in alignment with the precedence effect and natural atmospheric absorption. As elements move closer to the listener, the audio signal may transition between these pre-filtered distance channels, either abruptly or smoothly.

The result is that a scene with many localized sound-producing events may be auditorily rendered with a minimal number of playback or synthesis objects, each representing a spatial region rather than an individual source. This approach may be combined with traditional point-source audio generation, in which individual sounds may be attached to specific game objects and may be continuously looped or triggered by specific in-game events. The summary-driven and point-source approaches may operate simultaneously, with summary-driven audio providing the ambient spatial field and point-source audio providing discrete event feedback.

### I.O. Decoupled Tracking and Display Clocks with Per-Entity Adaptive Visual Interpolation

In a real-time vision-driven interactive system of the type described in this disclosure, ML inference produces tracking results at the camera frame rate, typically 30Hz on a consumer webcam, while the visual rendering loop runs at the display refresh rate, typically 60Hz to 120Hz or higher on a modern display. The ratio between the two rates means that for each new tracking sample, the rendering loop will produce multiple display frames. Conventional implementations either render the tracked position unchanged across all display frames between tracking samples, producing a visible stair-step pattern as the rendered position jumps once per tracking sample, or they invoke the inference pipeline at the display rate, producing duplicate inference work on identical pixels without producing new tracking information.

This disclosure describes an architectural pattern in which the gameplay logic and the visual rendering operate on two distinct clocks derived from a single tracking pipeline, and in which the visual rendering is interpolated across multiple display frames per tracking sample at a per-tracked-entity rate that adapts to each entity's current motion.

The two clocks are:

- **Gameplay clock** (the snap clock): updates at the tracking sample rate, for example via `requestVideoFrameCallback` or any equivalent mechanism that fires once per decoded camera frame. Any logic that must respond instantly to user input — for example pinch detection, gesture state transitions, projectile release, hit detection, scoring, or other gameplay state changes — reads tracking values directly from this clock with zero added latency. The gameplay-clock value is the most recent raw tracking sample, used as-is without interpolation toward any other state.

- **Visual clock** (the interpolation clock): updates at the display refresh rate, for example via `requestAnimationFrame` or any equivalent display-synchronized callback. Any element rendered on screen — for example the camera position for head-coupled parallax, the rendered hand or face wireframe, or the position of an object visually attached to a tracked point such as a held ball or visual indicator — reads from a separate per-entity displayed-position state that interpolates toward the latest gameplay-clock value over a configurable number of display frames.

The visual interpolation is performed by a linear ramp distributing the move from the previous displayed position to the latest gameplay-clock target value across N display frames. At step k of N, the displayed position advances by 1 / (N - k + 1) of the remaining distance to the target, producing a uniform stepwise ramp 1/N, 2/N, 3/N, ... N/N over the N display frames between consecutive gameplay-clock samples. When a new gameplay-clock sample arrives mid-ramp, the ramp is reset and a new ramp begins from the current displayed position toward the new target value, which gracefully handles the case where the display rate is not an exact integer multiple of the gameplay-clock rate.

The number of interpolation steps N is selected per tracked entity (for example head, hand left, hand right, individually) per gameplay-clock tick based on the entity's current motion speed, computed as the magnitude of the position delta between consecutive gameplay-clock samples. Three motion regimes are distinguished by speed thresholds:

- **At rest** (motion speed below a low threshold): N = 1, equivalent to instant snap to the latest gameplay-clock value. The system holds the snap-mode behavior for at-rest entities so that when motion subsequently begins, the first frame of the new motion is rendered at full magnitude rather than slowly ramping in from the rest position. This preserves the user's ability to perceive sudden motion initiations and direction changes without an interpolation-induced soft start.

- **Constant or near-constant motion** (motion speed above a transition threshold): N = N_max (for example 4), maximum interpolation. The system distributes each new sample's contribution across as many display frames as fit between consecutive gameplay-clock samples, producing visually smooth motion that updates every display frame even though the underlying tracking samples arrive at a much lower rate. The display rate's full temporal density of visual updates is delivered to the user, and the rendered motion appears as continuous gliding rather than as a sequence of position jumps.

- **Transitioning between rest and motion** (motion speed in the threshold band between rest and constant motion): N = an intermediate value (for example 2). This is a deliberately narrow speed slice that briefly catches the transitions in and out of motion before settling into either snap mode (at rest) or maximum interpolation mode (in motion).

Each tracked entity selects its own interpolation step count N independently of every other tracked entity. A still hand may be in N = 1 mode while a moving head is in N = N_max mode, and either may transition independently as their motion changes over time. The selection runs once per gameplay-clock tick per entity and the chosen N is used for the interpolation ramp until the next gameplay-clock tick for that entity. The motion speed used for the regime decision may be computed directly from the raw position delta or may be passed through an exponential moving average or other temporal smoother prior to threshold comparison, depending on the noise characteristics of the upstream tracking signal. For tracking pipelines whose upstream signal is sufficiently clean (for example after the application of techniques such as those described in II.G and II.H), no additional smoothing is required and the raw frame-to-frame speed delta may be used directly.

When a held object such as a virtual ball, projectile, or visual indicator is rendered at the position of a tracked hand, its rendered position uses the interpolated visual-clock hand position so that the held object glides with the same visual smoothness as the rendered hand wireframe. At the moment of release — for example when the user transitions from a pinching gesture to an open-hand gesture and the held object is launched as a projectile — the projectile's release origin is teleported to the raw gameplay-clock hand position rather than the interpolated visual-clock position, and the projectile's initial velocity may be derived from the raw gameplay-clock hand velocity. The discrepancy between the interpolated visual position and the raw gameplay position is small in display coordinates, typically a few pixels at most, and is perceptually invisible because the user's visual attention shifts from the held object to the launched projectile during the saccadic eye movement that accompanies the release. Saccadic suppression eliminates conscious visual perception of small position changes during such eye movements, so the small teleport from interpolated to raw position at the release moment is not perceived as a discontinuity by the user. The result is a release origin that is physically accurate to the user's actual hand position at the moment of release, with no visible discontinuity in the rendered scene.

The combination of these elements produces an interactive system in which gameplay-relevant actions (gesture detection, release moments, hit registration, scoring, and any other input-driven state changes) respond at the full gameplay-clock latency with no added rendering-induced delay, while the visually rendered scene updates smoothly at the full display refresh rate with motion-adaptive interpolation that emphasizes responsiveness during motion onset and visual smoothness during sustained motion. The user experiences instantaneous gameplay response while simultaneously experiencing smooth visual motion that updates at every available display frame, two perceptual properties that conventional fixed-rate tracking pipelines cannot deliver simultaneously.

This pattern may further be exposed to the user via a control element that selects between snap mode (N = 1 always), fixed interpolation modes (N = 2 or N = 4 always), and the per-entity adaptive mode described above, optionally with a real-time visualization of the chosen N per tracked entity for tuning and diagnostic purposes. The pattern applies to any tracking system in which an underlying detector or estimator runs at a lower rate than the display, including but not limited to ML keypoint output streams driving real-time interactive scenes, pose estimation pipelines, gesture recognition systems, or other vision-derived spatial signals consumed by a higher-rate visual rendering loop.

As of the date of this disclosure, the combination of bidirectional clock decoupling between gameplay state and visual rendering, per-entity adaptive interpolation rate driven by smoothed motion speed, and origin teleportation at gesture release moments to preserve physical accuracy under saccadic suppression, applied specifically to ML keypoint output streams driving GPU-rendered interactive scenes in a client-side runtime, is not known in any prior browser-based interactive system.

## Claim II. Zero-Readback GPU-Accelerated ML Pipeline for Real-Time Vision

A pipeline for deploying multi-stage ML vision models to GPU compute in a client-side runtime, in which model inference, inter-stage preprocessing, and intermediate data transfer remain GPU-resident, with only final outputs (for example landmark coordinates) crossing the GPU-CPU boundary. The pipeline encompasses model extraction from sealed upstream frameworks, conversion to a portable format (for example ONNX), execution via GPU compute shaders (for example WebGPU) in parallel off-thread execution contexts (for example Web Workers), and GPU-resident preprocessing between detection stages. As of the date of this disclosure, no other known system uses the pipeline architecture described herein, specifically WebGPU compute shaders with zero inter-stage CPU readback and parallel off-thread execution, to drive real-time vision tracking for interactive applications in a browser.

The pipeline is implemented as a standalone open-source library ([webgpu-vision](https://github.com/Sonified/webgpu-vision)) and applies to any multi-stage ML vision task deployable to GPU compute in a client-side runtime, including but not limited to hand tracking, face tracking, pose estimation, object detection, or other real-time vision applications.

### Technological context

The WebGPU API first shipped in a single browser in April 2023, with cross-browser availability not reached until November 2025, exposing dedicated GPU compute shader capability to web applications for the first time. Prior browser GPU access via WebGL was limited to the graphics pipeline, requiring workarounds such as fragment shader passes and texture readbacks to perform general-purpose computation.

ML inference workloads, including the neural networks used for face and hand landmark detection, are inherently parallelizable: they consist primarily of matrix multiplications and element-wise operations that map naturally to the massively parallel execution model of GPU compute shaders. Prior to WebGPU, browser-based ML inference relied on WebGL, which was designed for graphics rendering and requires synchronous CPU readback to extract computation results. With WebGPU's compute shader model, it is possible to eliminate this constraint, enabling ML inference to run entirely on the GPU with results remaining in GPU memory between pipeline stages.

Prior implementations route ML inference through the browser's main thread or through WebGL contexts that serialize against it, forcing inference and rendering to compete for the same sequential execution resource. Every millisecond spent on inference is a millisecond the rendering loop cannot advance a frame. By moving the entire inference workload to GPU compute shaders executing in parallel off-thread contexts, the disclosed pipeline decouples inference from rendering entirely: both proceed concurrently, and neither blocks the other.

### Perceptual requirements

Real-time vision-driven interaction systems, including but not limited to head-coupled parallax and gesture recognition, require both high rendering framerate and low tracking latency simultaneously. These are independent requirements that must both be met.

Empirical research has established approximately 120fps as the perceptual comfort threshold for interactive 3D applications, below which users experience increased simulator sickness symptoms and compensatory cognitive load (Wang, Shi et al., 2023, IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics). At 60fps, measurable nausea increase and active perceptual compensation are documented. VR platform requirements (for example Meta VRC.Quest.Performance.1) treat 60fps as a minimum acceptable floor, not a target.

Independently, head-coupled parallax is particularly sensitive to tracking latency because the user's own physical movement is the input signal: any delay between head motion and visual response is directly perceptible as image slip, where the scene appears to slide relative to head movement rather than remaining locked to the physical world. Research on head-tracking latency in parallax systems has established the just-noticeable difference at approximately 15ms or less (Mania et al., 2004, ACM Symposium on Applied Perception in Graphics and Visualization), and parallax stability degrades significantly when latency exceeds approximately 21ms (2024). VR platform manufacturers maintain strict motion-to-photon latency targets consistent with this research, for example under 18ms for Sony PSVR and 13ms at 90Hz for Meta headsets. Above these thresholds, the illusion of spatial presence collapses.

### Benchmark summary

Prior browser-based ML vision frameworks force an irreconcilable tradeoff between these two requirements due to architectural constraints in their threading and GPU access models (see II.A). The leading framework operating on the main thread achieves approximately 59fps, within the documented sickness-risk zone, while achieving low tracking latency. Moved to a Web Worker, the same framework achieves 120fps but at 53ms tracking latency, well above the perceptual fusion threshold. The disclosed pipeline achieves both 120fps rendering and approximately 10ms tracking latency simultaneously, where the leading framework can achieve either smooth framerate or low latency but not both (see II.A for detailed conditions and three-way comparison).

### II.A. Elimination of the Framerate-Latency Tradeoff in Browser-Based Vision Pipelines

In benchmarking prior browser-based ML vision frameworks, including sealed WASM-based inference binaries that advertise GPU acceleration, a consistent tradeoff between rendering framerate and tracking latency was observed across all configurations tested. Investigation of the underlying architecture revealed that this tradeoff arises from the interaction between browser threading constraints, WebGL context binding, and pixel data serialization, and is structural rather than tunable.

When inference runs on the main thread, the vision model has direct access to the video element and produces landmarks with low raw latency (for example approximately 25ms). However, synchronous inference blocks the rendering loop, reducing framerate to well below interactive thresholds (for example 59fps mean with dips to 29fps in measured conditions). This framerate falls below the 60fps minimum floor for interactive applications (Claypool et al., 2023, ACM CHI; Wang, Shi et al., 2023, IEEE TVCG).

The standard mitigation is to move inference to a Web Worker, which frees the rendering thread. However, workers cannot access DOM elements including the video source. Pixel data must be copied via createImageBitmap (approximately 5-10ms), transferred to the worker via postMessage, processed through WASM/WebGL inference (approximately 25ms), and results transferred back, with browser event loop scheduling delays at each thread boundary (approximately 2-5ms per crossing). The total round-trip measured in practice with two hands and one face tracked concurrently is approximately 53ms mean (78ms worst case), more than double the main-thread inference time. In benchmarking, the worker approach achieved smooth framerate (120fps) but tracking landmarks were stale by 53ms, producing visible lag between the user's physical hand and its rendered position. The latency is architectural: pixels live on the main thread, inference lives on the worker, and every crossing costs time that cannot be eliminated within this threading model.

A further architectural constraint prevents parallel execution of multiple vision tasks. The prior framework creates a single WebGL context internally, and all inference runs through it synchronously. Concurrent calls to separate detection tasks (for example hand landmark detection and face landmark detection) cannot overlap because WebGL contexts are not re-entrant: commands serialize on the GPU, and the framework's internal readback (for example glReadPixels) blocks the calling thread until completion. Even using asynchronous scheduling (for example Promise.all), the underlying WebGL operations execute sequentially. This means that in the prior framework, adding a second vision task (for example face tracking alongside hand tracking) adds its full inference time to the per-frame latency rather than running in parallel.

In testing, no configuration of the prior framework achieved both high framerate and low tracking latency simultaneously, nor was it able to parallelize multiple vision tasks. These limitations are architectural rather than computational, arising from the combination of single-threaded WebGL contexts, non-reentrant GPU access, DOM-bound video access, and cross-thread serialization overhead, and persisted on high-end hardware (see benchmark conditions below).

The following benchmarks were conducted on a MacBook Pro M1 Max (64GB RAM, 32-core GPU) running Chrome, with a full interactive scene including head-coupled parallax, two-hand tracking, and face detection running concurrently. The tradeoff persists on this high-end hardware, confirming that the limitation is architectural rather than computational:

| Configuration | FPS (mean) | FPS (min) | Hand latency (mean) | Hand latency (max) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MediaPipe main thread | 59 | 29 | 24.6ms | 26.6ms |
| MediaPipe Web Worker | 120 | 117 | 53.1ms | 78.2ms |
| WebGPU ONNX (disclosed) | 120 | 119 | 10.2ms | 12.1ms |

The main-thread configuration of the prior framework achieves low latency but drops framerate below the 60fps perceptual floor. The worker configuration of the prior framework recovers framerate but more than doubles latency to 53ms, well above the perceptual fusion threshold. The disclosed pipeline achieves both 120fps and 10ms latency simultaneously, a 5.2x latency reduction over the worker path.

The following table isolates the scaling behavior as vision tasks are added, demonstrating the effect of the prior framework's non-reentrant WebGL architecture (measured on the same hardware, main-thread configuration, with blendshape inference included, which extracts facial expression coefficients used for avatar animation and lip synchronization as described in I.J and I.K):

The prior framework's throughput degrades linearly with each added task because all tasks serialize through a single non-reentrant WebGL context. The disclosed pipeline maintains near-constant latency regardless of task count because each vision task may run in an independent execution context with its own GPU device. Notably, for the face + blendshapes workload critical to geometry-driven lip synchronization (I.K), the prior framework operates at 17.7ms, approaching the 22ms film-quality lip sync threshold, while the disclosed pipeline operates at 11.1ms, well within it.

The disclosed pipeline resolves the tradeoff by running inference directly on the GPU via WebGPU compute shaders, without crossing thread boundaries for pixel data. GPU buffer objects written by one stage are passed directly to the next via zero-copy references (for example via Tensor.fromGpuBuffer()), without returning to CPU. This eliminates the ImageBitmap creation, cross-thread postMessage serialization, and WASM/WebGL context overhead present in prior approaches. The only CPU readback in the complete pipeline is the final landmark output (measured at 252 bytes per hand and 5.7KB per face per frame in the benchmarked configuration). The result is 5.2x lower latency than the worker path and 2x lower than main-thread inference, while maintaining 120fps rendering throughout.

### II.B. GPU-Side Preprocessing Between Detection Stages

While II.A addresses the readback bottleneck during inference itself, a separate source of CPU-GPU roundtrips exists in the preprocessing steps between inference stages. Even a pipeline with zero-readback inference may still bounce data through the CPU for preprocessing, negating the latency gains. Standard browser vision pipelines perform preprocessing steps such as letterbox padding and affine warping by routing image data through CPU-accessible surfaces such as Canvas between detection stages. This CPU-GPU-CPU roundtrip may dominate preprocessing latency in multi-stage pipelines even when inference itself is fast. In the disclosed pipeline, these preprocessing steps may be performed entirely via GPU compute shaders or equivalent GPU-side operations, keeping image data GPU-resident between stages such as palm detection and landmark inference. The preprocessed tensor may be passed directly to the next inference stage via zero-copy GPU buffer reference or equivalent mechanism, consistent with the pipeline architecture described in II.A. Combined with II.A, this ensures that image data entering the pipeline from the camera never leaves the GPU until final landmark output.

### II.C. Complementary Kernel Fusion as Workaround for WebGPU Serial Dispatch

WebGPU's underlying browser implementations (for example the Dawn backend used in Chromium-based browsers) hardcode serial GPU dispatch, preventing async compute and multi-queue execution. This is not a missing feature that may be added in a future release; it is an architectural constraint in the Metal command buffer implementation (for example MTLDispatchTypeSerial in Dawn's CommandBufferMTL.mm). Every WebGPU application in every browser is limited to executing GPU compute dispatches sequentially. Native GPU APIs such as Vulkan and Direct3D 12 overcome this limitation via multi-queue async compute, allowing independent workloads to overlap on the GPU. This path is unavailable in WebGPU.

This disclosure describes a technique that achieves GPU workload overlap within this constraint by fusing operations with complementary memory access and execution patterns into a single compute shader dispatch. When one operation stalls on a resource (for example memory latency from scattered reads across cache-line boundaries) while another uses a different resource (for example ALU computation or sequential buffer writes), executing both within the same shader allows the GPU's wavefront scheduler to fill stall gaps automatically, achieving overlap that serial multi-dispatch cannot.

This technique was empirically benchmarked on realistic workloads (Apple M1, Chrome, WebGPU, 2026-04-04). In a voxel cellular automaton simulation with 7 element types, collision matrix, momentum diffusion, mass-weighted gravity, element decay, particle advection, spell injection, and active cell scanning, fusion of the CA neighbor traversal (scattered reads, heavy cache misses on grids exceeding L2 cache) with the gravity field update (same neighbor traversal, sequential force vector writes) reduced total simulation time by 1.19x to 1.75x depending on grid size. The fusion gain scales with grid size because larger grids exceed L2 cache capacity, creating more memory stall cycles for sequential writes to fill.

Benchmarked against alternative parallelization approaches on the same hardware and workload: multi-device WebGPU (peak 1.98x with overhead), multi-worker dispatch (1.30x), and kernel fusion (1.19x-1.75x on real pipeline, 2.14x on synthetic complementary workloads). Single-shader fusion on a single device with zero dispatch overhead matched or exceeded the alternatives.

For the game simulation described in the companion integer simulation disclosure, all three standard grid configurations (0.5M, 1.0M, and 2.1M cells) complete the entire simulation tick, including CA, gravity, particles, spell injection, and active cell scan, in under 42% of the 120fps frame budget on M1 hardware without sparse compute optimization. Kernel fusion is the technique that makes the next tier of grid resolution viable: a 4.2M-cell grid drops from 83% of frame budget (at risk) to 68% (comfortable) with fusion.

More generally, this technique may apply to any pair of GPU operations where one is memory-bound and the other is compute-bound, including but not limited to: voxel neighbor reads fused with cell state writes, BVH tree traversal fused with framebuffer writes, gravity field sampling fused with element table lookups, particle advection fused with particle position writes, or ML preprocessing (memory-bound texture sampling) fused with the first inference layer (ALU-bound matrix multiply).

Kernel fusion does not apply uniformly. Operations that are both ALU-bound (for example ML inference layers, which achieve high arithmetic intensity) do not benefit because there are no memory stall gaps to fill. The appropriate architectural strategy may vary by workload: kernel fusion for complementary stall patterns, Web Worker parallelism for CPU-side concurrency (as described in Claim II), or sequential execution where data dependencies require it. As of the date of this disclosure, this is the first documented application of complementary kernel fusion as a workaround for WebGPU's lack of async compute.

### II.D. Multi-Device GPU Overlap via Separate Adapter Handles

WebGPU's device model consumes the adapter handle on first device creation, preventing multiple devices from sharing a single adapter. By requesting separate adapters via multiple calls to navigator.gpu.requestAdapter() and creating independent GPU devices from each, the system may achieve measurable GPU work overlap when the workloads target different GPU functional units. For example, a texture-sampling workload on one device and a memory-copy workload on another device may overlap via the hardware's wavefront scheduler because they contend for different resources.

Benchmarked on identical hardware (Apple M1, Chrome, WebGPU, 2026-04-04): compute + compute workloads targeting the same functional unit achieved 1.01x overlap (no benefit, as expected). Texture + DMA workloads across two devices achieved 1.35x. Three devices across three workers achieved 1.53x. DMA + memory workloads achieved 1.64x mean with 1.98x peak. The overlap is workload-dependent and scales with functional unit diversity, not device count alone.

This technique complements the kernel fusion approach of II.C: fusion is preferred when workloads have complementary stall patterns within a single dispatch, while multi-device overlap may be preferred when workloads are independently schedulable and target different GPU functional units. Both techniques work within WebGPU's serial dispatch constraint.

### II.E. Op Decomposition for GPU Runtime Compatibility

When a GPU-based ML runtime lacks a native kernel for a given operation, the runtime falls back to CPU execution, pulling data off the GPU, computing on the CPU, and pushing it back. This disclosure describes a pre-deployment graph transformation that decomposes unsupported operations into compositions of GPU-native ops using equivalent mathematical identities, such that every operation in the modified graph has a native GPU implementation and no CPU fallback is triggered. The result is zero CPU-GPU roundtrips for the decomposed operations, zero accuracy loss, and full GPU inference speed recovered.

This technique applies to any neural network model containing ops unsupported by a target runtime and is not limited to any single operation or model type. For example, ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) is a portable model format that enables models trained in one framework to be executed in another runtime; models converted to ONNX may be executed on WebGPU via runtimes such as ONNX Runtime Web, but may encounter unsupported ops in the target runtime's GPU execution provider. Any unsupported op that can be expressed as a mathematically equivalent composition of supported primitives may be decomposed in this manner. For browser-based ML and other environments where runtime op coverage lags behind native frameworks, this is a practical path to deploying models that would otherwise be GPU-unusable, keeping the entire inference pipeline on the GPU.

### II.F. PReLU Decomposition for WebGPU Execution

As a specific application of II.E, a neural network model may contain multiple instances of an unsupported activation function, each of which independently triggers a GPU-to-CPU fallback. For example, in one embodiment, a widely deployed face landmark model contains 69 PReLU layers, each triggering a CPU fallback when executed via a WebGPU-based inference runtime, reducing throughput to approximately 9fps (110ms per frame) despite all other operators executing on GPU. Decomposing each instance of the unsupported operation into a composition of GPU-native ops using the method of II.E eliminates all such fallbacks, recovering full GPU inference speed. The source model may originate from any upstream framework (for example extracted from a sealed task bundle and converted from TFLite to ONNX via format conversion tools such as tf2onnx), and the decomposition may be applied to any activation function or other operation type that lacks native GPU kernel support in the target runtime. Without this transformation, the face landmark model used for head-coupled parallax and lip synchronization in Claims I.A and I.J would be unable to run at interactive frame rates on WebGPU, and the perceptual coherence thresholds described in Claim II would not be met.

### II.G. Multi-Output Regressor Utilization for Sub-Pixel Spatial Localization

Object detection models commonly produce multiple spatial predictions per detection from a single forward pass through a shared backbone, including but not limited to a bounding box (cx, cy, w, h) and a small set of object-specific keypoints (for example facial landmarks such as eyes, nose, mouth, and ears in a face detection model, or palm keypoints in a hand detection model). Each spatial output is produced by a distinct regressor head with its own learned weights, and although these heads share earlier convolutional features, their final-layer quantization noise is statistically independent in the limit. Conventional consumers of such models typically use only one of the available spatial outputs (most commonly the bounding box center for the model's primary task, or a single named keypoint such as a fingertip or eye), discarding the remaining outputs. This limits the spatial precision of the consumer to the noise floor of a single regressor head.

This disclosure describes a technique in which the consumer of a multi-output detection model fuses two or more of the model's spatial outputs into a single position estimate by averaging or otherwise combining the predictions, intra-frame, before the result is passed to downstream consumers such as smoothing filters, gameplay logic, or rendering. Because the constituent outputs come from independent regressor heads with statistically independent quantization noise, the noise floor of the fused estimate decreases by approximately the square root of the number of fused signals, while the bias of the estimate is preserved if the individual heads are individually unbiased. For example, fusing the bounding box center with all six keypoints from a face detection model produces seven spatial signals from a single inference call, reducing the noise floor of the resulting position estimate by approximately sqrt(7) ≈ 2.6x without any change to the model itself, without any temporal averaging, and at a per-frame cost of a small constant number of floating-point additions and a single division.

This technique is particularly significant for detection models that operate at low input resolutions, for example 128x128 or smaller, where the per-pixel quantization of each spatial prediction is large relative to the desired tracking precision in display-space coordinates. In such cases, the fused estimate may achieve sub-pixel display-space precision from a model whose individual spatial outputs are integer-pixel quantized at the model input resolution. The visible effect is the elimination of stair-step jitter on slow movements that would otherwise be perceptible to a user observing the tracked position.

This is to be distinguished from temporal averaging techniques, including but not limited to moving averages, exponential filters, and One Euro filters, which combine measurements taken at different moments in time and necessarily introduce latency proportional to the smoothing window. The technique described here is purely intra-frame: all signals being averaged were produced by the same inference call on the same input frame, so the fused estimate has the same end-to-end latency as a single-output approach. The model was already computing all of the regressor outputs as part of its existing forward pass; the technique consumes work the model was already performing rather than adding any additional inference computation.

The technique applies to any detection model that produces two or more spatial predictions per detection from a single forward pass, including but not limited to face detection models with bounding box plus facial keypoints, hand detection models with bounding box plus palm keypoints, object detection models with bounding box plus corners or anchor points, multi-keypoint pose estimation models, or any other multi-output regression architecture. Different fusion strategies (uniform averaging, confidence-weighted averaging, geometric subset selection, or fusion only of outputs known a priori to have similar bias) may be selected based on the specific model and the spatial relationships among its outputs.

### II.H. Detection-Cascade ROI Tracking with Training-Distribution Preservation

Single-stage object detection models, including but not limited to BlazeFace and similar lightweight face or object detectors, are conventionally deployed by running the model on the full camera frame each tick, without exploiting the temporal continuity between consecutive frames in a video stream. This conventional deployment exhibits two failure modes. First, the detector's effective spatial resolution is consumed by background pixels that do not contain the object of interest, reducing the precision with which the object can be localized in display coordinates. Second, the detector's per-frame output exhibits frame-to-frame jitter even when the underlying object has not moved, because anchor matching, score thresholding, and weighted suppression produce slightly different outputs as background details vary across frames.

This disclosure describes an ROI tracking technique applied to the detection stage itself, in which the input to the detector on each subsequent frame is a cropped region of the camera frame around the previous frame's detected bounding box, rather than the full frame. After a successful detection at one tick, the system computes a crop region centered on the detected bbox and scaled by a margin factor, and on the next tick provides only that cropped region as input to the detector. The detector's output spatial coordinates are then mapped back to full-frame coordinates by undoing the crop offset and scale before passing them to downstream consumers. If the detector returns no detections from the cropped region (the object has moved out of the cropped window), the system falls back to running the detector on the full frame on the next tick, re-acquiring the object. This produces a self-tracking detection cascade in which the detector "talks to itself" across consecutive frames, structurally analogous to the cascade pattern used between separate detection and landmark stages in two-stage tracking architectures, but applied here to a single detector across consecutive frames of the same camera stream.

A critical aspect of this technique is the explicit preservation of the model's training-distribution input fill fraction. The detection model was trained on frames in which the target object occupies a particular fraction of the input — for example, face detection models such as BlazeFace are typically trained on faces that occupy approximately 25-30% of the input frame area. If the crop margin is set tightly around the object's bounding box, the cropped object fills a substantially larger fraction of the model input than the model expects, pushing the input outside the model's training distribution. Empirically, when the cropped input fill rises substantially above the training distribution fill, detection models exhibit qualitative failure modes including but not limited to detecting subregions of the object (for example interpreting the eye region of a face as a new face) or producing wildly inconsistent detections across frames. The crop margin must therefore be selected to keep the cropped object's input fill within the model's training distribution range, not simply tight enough to encompass the bounding box.

For a face detection model trained on faces occupying approximately 28% of the input frame, a crop margin of approximately 3.5x the larger of the bounding box dimensions produces a cropped input where the face occupies approximately 28% of the model input regardless of the actual object size in the source frame. A minimum absolute crop dimension may additionally be enforced so that small or distant objects produce a sufficiently large cropped input window for stable detection regardless of the source object size. The exact margin factor and minimum dimensions may be determined empirically per model by observing the detector's output stability as a function of crop margin, with the working range corresponding to the input fill fraction at which the model was trained.

This technique provides three benefits simultaneously. First, the input to the detector is dramatically more stable frame-to-frame, because the cropped region is centered and sized consistently with respect to the tracked object, which eliminates most of the per-frame jitter that arises from the detector's anchor-matching variation on differing inputs. Second, the model's effective spatial resolution is concentrated on pixels that contain the object of interest rather than wasted on background, improving the precision of the detector's spatial output. Third, the per-frame compute cost of the camera-to-model data path decreases because the cropped input is smaller than the full frame, reducing the cost of bitmap creation, GPU upload, and any preprocessing steps applied prior to inference. The reduction in input pixels does not reduce the cost of inference itself when the model has a fixed input resolution that is letterboxed or resized, but it does reduce all per-pixel preprocessing costs upstream of the model.

The technique applies to any detection model with a known or estimable training-distribution input fill fraction, including but not limited to face detection, hand detection, object detection, animal detection, pose estimation, or other single-stage detection models. The combination of detection-cascade ROI tracking and the multi-output spatial averaging of II.G is particularly powerful when applied together to the same model: ROI tracking provides a stable input window to the detector (input-side stability), and multi-output spatial averaging extracts maximum positional precision from each output (output-side stability), producing tracking quality that is substantially better than either technique alone.

## Claim III. In-Browser GPU-Accelerated Gesture Model Training and Real-Time Deployment

A system for defining, training, and deploying recognition models for gestures and poses derived from body-relative landmarks, including but not limited to hand gestures, body poses, facial expressions, or other landmark-based inputs, entirely within a client-side runtime such as a browser, with no server dependency for any stage of the training or inference pipeline. The complete training pipeline — feature extraction, forward pass, loss computation, gradient accumulation, and weight update — executes on the client GPU via compute shaders. The trained model is available for real-time inference within the same session at interactive frame rates. ### III.A. Derived Geometric Feature Families

Raw hand landmark coordinates from a single RGB camera vary with hand size, camera distance, hand position within the frame, and individual anatomy, making them unsuitable as direct classifier input without normalization. Transforming raw coordinates into derived geometric features produces a representation that is stable across these sources of variation, enabling a classifier trained on one user's hands to generalize across sessions, distances, and camera positions.

Hand landmark coordinates may be transformed into a computed geometric feature vector combining multiple orthogonal feature families, including but not limited to: inter-fingertip contact distances (for example 10 features from all pairwise fingertip combinations), thumb-to-joint distances for relative finger positioning (for example 12 features), per-joint flexion angles measuring curl at each knuckle (for example 15 features), inter-finger spread angles (for example 4 features), palm openness as a scalar (for example 1 feature), and camera-relative palm orientation angles (for example 3 features). Shape features are normalized by palm size for scale invariance and computed relative to the hand's own geometry for rotation invariance, while orientation features preserve camera-relative direction to distinguish poses that differ only in palm facing direction, such as poses as subtle as ASL signs U and H, or P and K.
The resulting feature vector (for example 45 features in one configuration) serves as input to a classifier, such as a multilayer perceptron or other suitable model, trained to map specific hand poses to discrete trigger events, including for example casting a spell, activating an ability, or selecting an action. 

### III.B. Atomic Float Gradient Accumulation for On-Device GPU Training

GPU-based gesture training may use an atomic accumulation technique to accumulate gradients from all training samples in parallel into a single weight-sized buffer. Specifically, WGSL provides atomic operations only on 32-bit unsigned integers. Float gradient accumulation is achieved by reinterpreting float values as unsigned integers via bitcast, performing an atomic compare-and-swap loop (atomicCompareExchangeWeak), and bitcasting back. This enables all training samples to accumulate gradients into a single shared weight buffer in parallel, without per-sample gradient storage.

Standard GPU training allocates per-sample gradient arrays and then reduces them in a second pass. This approach requires memory proportional to the number of weights only (for example 111KB for a 27,776-weight classifier), rather than proportional to the number of training samples multiplied by the number of weights (for example 1.3GB for 13,000 training samples under full-batch gradient descent, or 27MB under mini-batch). This represents a memory reduction of over four orders of magnitude for typical gesture training workloads, making real-time on-device gesture model training practical in a browser tab or comparable client-side runtime.

### III.C. ML Recognition Confidence as Continuous Visible Gameplay Variable

In gesture-driven interactive systems, the recognition confidence score produced by an ML model or other classification system may be exposed as a continuous, real-time visible gameplay variable rather than consumed silently as a binary accept/reject threshold. In one implementation, gesture recognition confidence drives in-game quantities such as spell intensity, shield strength, or ability power, giving the player direct real-time feedback on physical input quality and creating a closed-loop skill acquisition system driven by the model's output.

This approach may be distinguished from binary gesture acceptance, where confidence is consumed internally and the player sees only a recognized or rejected outcome, and from post-hoc scoring as in rhythm games, where input quality is evaluated after the fact. The confidence signal may instead be made continuous, visible, and actionable during the input itself.

In traditional game design, spell failure or degraded ability outcomes are typically governed by random number generation: dice rolls, percentage chances, or pseudorandom draws that the player cannot directly influence through physical skill. In this system, ML model confidence may replace random number generation as the governing variable for outcome variance. A spell fizzles not because a random number fell below a threshold, but because the model's confidence in the detected gesture was low, which in turn reflects the physical clarity of the player's hand pose, lighting conditions, occlusion, or distance from the camera. Unlike a dice roll, this source of variance is learnable: a player may practice gesture form, adjust hand position, or improve lighting to increase model confidence, converting what would traditionally be opaque randomness into a trainable physical skill. Model limitations such as landmark dropout at frame edges, reduced confidence for occluded fingers, or sensitivity to hand orientation may function as the equivalent of environmental gameplay factors that a skilled player learns to manage. The limitations of the ML model become the physics of the game world.

This principle may extend to input vocabulary design: core mechanics may be intentionally mapped to input poses or patterns that maximize model confidence, co-designing the interaction vocabulary with the model's confidence topology such that model strengths become gameplay advantages and model limitations become legible skill feedback rather than invisible failures. This approach applies to any recognition modality, including but not limited to hand pose, body pose, facial expression, or voice command recognition systems.

In a language acquisition context, for example American Sign Language, the same continuous recognition confidence signal that governs gameplay outcomes may be exposed as a visual or auditory feedback channel, providing a learner with real-time indication of how closely their hand pose matches a target sign, enabling self-directed skill acquisition without instructor feedback. The underlying ML model becomes a mirror: the learner continuously adjusts their form in response to a continuously updating signal until the model's confidence reflects correct execution, whether the target behavior is a game mechanic or a handshape in a visual language.
